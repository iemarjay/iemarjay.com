## Advice Frameworks

Reusable frameworks I share with mentees. These are battle-tested from my own experience and refined through sessions.

### Interview Preparation

#### The 3-Project Portfolio Rule
Don't show 10 half-finished repos. Show 3 projects that demonstrate:
- **One that solves YOUR problem** — shows initiative and genuine interest
- **One with other users/contributors** — shows you can collaborate
- **One that's deployed and live** — shows you ship, not just build

#### "Explain Like a Debugger"
When asked technical questions, walk through your thought process out loud:
- "First I'd check if..."
- "That tells me..."
- "So I'd try..."

Interviewers care more about HOW you think than getting the right answer. Silence is your enemy.

#### The Failure Story Framework
Every developer should have one genuine failure story ready:
1. **What happened** (brief, own it)
2. **What you learned** (specific, technical)
3. **What you do differently now** (actionable change)

This shows maturity. Most candidates only talk about wins — a well-told failure story stands out.

*My example: The $10k webhook replay attack that taught me to build systematic security checks.*

### Resume Crafting

#### Impact Over Tasks
❌ "Built REST API endpoints"
✅ "Built REST API that reduced page load time by 40%"

No metrics? Use specifics:
✅ "Built REST API handling 500+ daily requests for user authentication"

Hiring managers skim. Impact jumps off the page; tasks don't.

#### The GitHub Trap
Hiring managers WILL click your GitHub link. What they see in 3 seconds matters more than your commit history:
- Pinned repos should have READMEs with screenshots
- Your profile README should exist and be professional
- Green contribution squares matter less than quality repos

#### One Page, Zero Excuses
At entry/mid level, your resume is one page. Period. If you can't fit it, you're including things that don't matter for the role you're targeting.

### Effective Communication

#### The STAR-L Method
Standard STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) + **Lesson**:
- Most candidates stop at Result
- Adding what you'd do differently shows growth mindset
- The Lesson is what interviewers remember

#### Mirror the Interviewer
If they're technical, go deep. If they're asking high-level, stay high-level. Reading the room is an underrated skill. Match their energy and vocabulary.

#### Questions > Answers
The questions YOU ask reveal more than your answers:
- "What does success look like in 6 months?"
- "What's the biggest challenge the team is facing?"
- "How do you handle code reviews here?"

**Bad question**: "What's the salary?" (save for offer stage)

### Job Search Navigation

#### The 70% Rule
Apply if you meet 70% of requirements. Job postings are wish lists, not checklists. Companies list their ideal candidate knowing they won't find them.

#### Warm > Cold
One referral = 10 cold applications. Spend time:
- Engaging with company employees on Twitter/LinkedIn before applying
- Contributing to their open source repos
- Attending their meetups/events
- Asking for informational chats (not jobs)

#### The Rejection Reframe
Every "no" is data:
- Ask for feedback (50% will actually respond)
- Track patterns (always failing system design? Study that specifically)
- One "yes" is all you need — it's a numbers game with skill

#### Timing Matters
- Apply Monday-Wednesday morning (recruiters are freshest)
- Apply late Sunday night (top of inbox Monday morning)
- Avoid month-end (hiring freezes, budget reviews)
- January and September are high-hiring months

### Getting Discovered (Digital Presence)

#### The Discovery Principle
You only get hired when someone looking to hire finds you at the moment they're hiring. This means:
- **Position yourself where hiring managers are** — LinkedIn, Twitter, GitHub
- **Be searchable** — when someone searches "React developer" or "C# backend," your name should appear
- **Post consistently** — every post is a lottery ticket for discovery

The job isn't finding jobs. The job is being findable.

#### The Fresh Grad Remote Reality
International companies hiring remotely aren't looking for beginners. They're looking for skills they can't find in their own market.

**Why this matters:**
- Google has plenty of junior engineers in San Francisco
- If Google hires remotely from India, they want senior talent
- Fresh grads compete better in local markets first

**The path:**
1. Get local experience (even if pay is low)
2. Build public credibility (projects, posts, presence)
3. Then target international remote roles

#### The Experience Trade-off
Early career reality: **What companies don't give you in pay, they give you in experience.**

- Low-paying local job solving hard problems > no job waiting for perfect opportunity
- 6-12 months of real experience makes you hireable internationally
- Experience compounds — your second job will pay more because of your first

*"Get in the door. Learn everything. Level up. Then negotiate from strength."*

### Interview Communication

#### Interview Calibration
Interviews = bragging humbly. But calibrate to your audience:

**Technical interviewer:**
- Go deep on technicalities
- Mention specific problems: "I dealt with the N+1 query problem..."
- Show you speak their language

**HR/Non-technical interviewer:**
- Focus on soft skills
- Communication clarity, professionalism, cultural fit
- They won't understand technical depth anyway

**"Tell me about yourself" framework:**
- State who you are and your focus area
- Mention relevant experience/projects for THIS specific role
- End with why you're excited about this opportunity

   - LeetCode should be your daily friend
   - Practice consistently to crush technical rounds

4. **"Any strategies for job searching effectively?"**
   - Networking is #1 — befriend people in target industries
   - Engage with their posts, show what you're building
   - They can refer you and help skip the queue

5. **"How to communicate effectively in interviews?"**

#### Self-Review & Improvement Notes

**Session Rating:**
| Area | Score | Notes |
|------|-------|-------|
| Advice quality | 7/10 | Good frameworks, realistic perspective |
| Delivery | 5/10 | Too much monologue, not enough dialogue |

**What I did well:**
- Core frameworks are solid (Discovery Principle, Experience Trade-off)
- Didn't sugarcoat the fresh grad remote reality
- Offered concrete resources (Nethermind, Wikipedia internships)
- Good closing: "The best way to thank me is to land a job"

**What I need to improve:**

1. **Talked too much, listened too little**
   - 80% me talking. Should be 70% mentee in first 10 mins.
   - Ask: "What have you tried?" "What's blocking you?" "What's your 6-month goal?"

2. **Didn't dig into his actual work**
   - He has C# experience but I never asked to see his GitHub
   - Next time: "Send me your GitHub link right now, let's look together"

3. **Structure was scattered**
   - Jumped between digital presence, Web2/Web3, DSA, networking, interviews
   - Next time: Pick 2-3 topics max per session, go deep not wide

4. **"Just network" is incomplete advice**
   - Told him to "engage with posts" without showing HOW
   - Next time: "Comment on 5 posts/day. DM template: 'I'm exploring X, would you be open to a 15-min chat?'"

5. **Punted on the communication question**
   - Said "not enough time" instead of giving ONE actionable tip
   - Always give something, even if time is short

6. **No deadlines on action items**
   - "Build projects" and "post on LinkedIn" are too vague
   - Next time: "Before our next call, I want to see: 1 LinkedIn post, 1 improved README"

7. **Sensed he wasn't keeping up but kept going**
   - Asked "Am I speaking too fast?" then didn't slow down
   - Next time: Stop and ask "What's unclear right now?"

8. **"Take a local job" framing could feel dismissive**
   - Better: "Local experience is a STRATEGY to get remote jobs faster, not a fallback"

**Action items for myself (next session):**
- [ ] Start with questions, not advice (first 10 mins = mentee talking)
- [ ] Pick 2 topics max, go deep
- [ ] Look at mentee's actual work (GitHub, LinkedIn, resume)
- [ ] End with 1-2 concrete action items WITH deadlines
- [ ] Ask "What's your main takeaway?" before ending
